plainName: Asking the department to look at it again
oneLine: Often the first step, and usually the cheapest one.
order: 5
appliesTo:

whatItMeans: >-
  Many schemes let you ask the department to look at its own decision again.
  Often the person who looks again was not involved the first time. It is
  usually free, and it can be quicker than a tribunal or a court. In some
  schemes it is also the step you have to take before you can go anywhere else.
whatItIsNot: >-
  It is not an outside review. The same department is deciding, so it will not
  suit every situation. It also does not always pause the clock for the next
  step, so keep track of any other time limit while you wait.
keyPoints:
  - >-
    Your decision letter is the place to look. It usually sets out what you can
    ask for and by when.
  - >-
    The rules are different for every department, so there is no single answer
    about how it works.
  - It is usually free, and it may be quicker than an outside review.
  - >-
    For some schemes you have to do this first before an outside body will look
    at your case.
  - >-
    If the letter does not mention it, you can ask the department whether an
    internal review is available.

Added 2026-08-22 at the owner's direction. The app modelled merits review and judicial review and
skipped the step most people actually take first.

**This entry deliberately states no rule.** Internal review is created by each scheme's own Act or
policy, so there is no general provision to cite and no general time limit to state. Anything more
specific than "your letter will tell you, and the rules differ by department" would be invented.
What the entry can honestly do is make sure nobody skips a free, fast step because the app never
mentioned it.

**The letter is the answer, and the entry says so first.** A decision letter that offers internal
review almost always names the step and the period. Pointing a reader at the document already in
their hand beats any general statement this app could make.

Two warnings carried in `whatItIsNot`, both about things a reader would otherwise assume: the same
department is deciding, and asking does not necessarily stop another clock running.

`order: 5` puts it ahead of every other concept, because in the sequence people actually follow it
comes first.

**Two universal claims hedged 2026-08-23**, on the external legal review.

"Most departments will look at their own decision again" became "Many schemes let you ask". Some
schemes have no internal review at all, and the entry's own second `keyPoint` already says the
rules differ by department — so the opening sentence was promising something the rest of the page
would not stand behind.

The independence sentence was the real defect. "Someone who was not involved the first time takes
another look" was stated flatly, with nothing behind it but the knowledge base, and independence
is exactly what varies between schemes. It now reads "Often the person who looks again was not
involved the first time". A reader who is told to expect a fresh pair of eyes, and gets the
original decision-maker, has been set up to trust a step they should be checking.

"For many schemes" in the same field became "In some schemes", matching the `keyPoint` that
already said "For some schemes". Two sentences on one page should not put the same fact at two
different strengths.

**The speed claims hedged 2026-08-23**, on the external legal review, reversing the ruling in the
paragraph above. "Usually much quicker than a tribunal or a court" is now "can be quicker", and the
`keyPoint` "usually faster than any outside review" is now "may be quicker than an outside review".

"Usually" was thought to be hedge enough. It is not. We hold no comparative timing data for any
scheme, so a prevalence claim about speed had nothing behind it but the knowledge base.

The `keyPoint` was also the wider of the two. "Any outside review" sweeps in an Ombudsman complaint,
which this corpus nowhere describes as slow, so the two sentences were also stating the same fact at
two different strengths.

`oneLine` went with them, beyond what the review asked. It said "usually the fastest and cheapest
one", which is the same claim again and the strongest version of it, on the string a reader meets
first. It now says "usually the cheapest one". Hedging two fields and leaving the headline promising
speed would have put the contradiction where it does the most work.

"Usually free" and "usually the cheapest" stay as they are. Cost was not raised, and it is the half
of the pair the knowledge base does stand behind.
