  There are two separate things you can ask for, and people often mix them up.
  You can ask the decision-maker for a written statement of reasons. That tells
  you why. You can also make a freedom of information request. That asks to see
  documents. Asking for reasons is usually the more useful first step, and it is
  free.

  A freedom of information request is not a way to have a decision reviewed.
  Neither is a request for reasons. They get you information, and information is
  what tells you whether a review is worth it and what to say.

The mind map's fourth top-level avenue, split into the half our materials cover and the half they
do not.

**The reasons half is grounded.** The Combined Framework calls a statutory reasons request the
shared first step of either path — "A statutory reasons request (ADJR s 13; ALA s 8 in Vic) is
often the first step in either path" — and the Venture brief notes such requests "are under-used
because people don't know they exist", which is precisely the gap this node closes.

**The freedom-of-information half is not.** None of the four documents contains FOI doctrine,
procedure or case law; the only mention anywhere is a volume figure. So this entry states the Acts
by name and that charges may apply, and nothing further: **no deadline, no fee, no section number,
no exemptions, no process detail.** None of that is available from a source this app trusts.

The two requests are kept visually separate throughout, because a reader who conflates them will
ask the wrong body for the wrong thing and lose weeks doing it.

## Provenance

**s 8 verified against the Act itself (2026-08-19).** The four documents disagreed — three
statements gave the Victorian reasons right as s 8, the JR Hypo gave it as s 10. Checked against
the *Administrative Law Act 1978* (Vic): **s 8 is "Reasons for decision to be furnished by tribunal
on request by party concerned"**; **s 10 is "Reasons to be part of record"**. The Hypo's reference
is a slip. Sourced to the Act rather than to the secondary materials.

**Supervising lawyer's review, applied 2026-08-19.** Four refinements, all from one principle: do
not overstate a legal proposition where the source material itself acknowledges a limit.

- *Naming the review bodies.* "Federally the OAIC handles that" implied the Commissioner was both
  the first review step and the whole pathway. Neither is sourced. They are now described as
  "common review bodies for FOI decisions".
- *The Acts and charges.* Approved: the Acts may be named, and charges mentioned in general terms.
  No figures.
- *Internal review — nothing added, and that is the decision.* The draft asked the lawyer to
  confirm such a step existed and what it was called, which is an admission it was unverified.
  Under this app's own rule that is a reason to say nothing. No statement about an internal-review
  stage appears anywhere in this entry, and none should be added until it can be sourced.
- *Where the qualification sits.* s 8 places the duty on a "tribunal", which s 2 defines
  functionally — "a person or body of persons who ... is or are by law required ... to act in a
  judicial manner to the extent of observing one or more of the rules of natural justice". Broad,
  but not every decision-maker. Naming that term in customer copy would mislead badly, because a
  reader hears VCAT. The first draft kept "the decision-maker" and appended a caveat; the lawyer
  moved the qualification **into the core statement instead**, on the ground that a reader takes
  the first sentence and stops. So the copy now reads "In many cases you can ask the decision-maker
  for written reasons. Whether there is a legal duty to give them depends on the type of decision."
