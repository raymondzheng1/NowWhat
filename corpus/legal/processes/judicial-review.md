id: judicial-review
name: Judicial review
plainName: Asking a court to check the decision was made lawfully
oneLine: >-
  A court checks whether the decision was made the right way — not whether it
  was a good decision.
question: Was the decision made lawfully?
whatItIs: >-
  Judicial review is different from merits review. A court does not ask whether
  the decision was right or fair — it asks whether it was made lawfully: did the
  decision-maker have the power, follow a fair process, and stay within the law?
  If the court finds a legal error — a 'ground of review' — it can set the
  decision aside. But the court cannot make a new decision for you. It usually
  sends the matter back to be decided again, properly.
bodies:
  - jurisdiction: Cth
    name: The Federal Court / Federal Circuit and Family Court
    note: >-
      judicial review of Australian Government decisions (ADJR Act; s 39B
      Judiciary Act).
  - jurisdiction: Cth
    name: The High Court
    note: >-
      constitutional judicial review of Commonwealth officers (s 75(v)
      Constitution).
  - jurisdiction: Vic
    name: The Supreme Court of Victoria
    note: >-
      the Victorian equivalent, for state government decisions. There are two
      ways in, and they have different time limits, so this is worth asking a
      free legal service about early.
canApply:
  - >-
    You need a 'ground of review' — a specific legal error in how the decision
    was made.
  - You usually need to be a person affected by the decision (standing).
  - >-
    There are strict time limits, and they are usually NOT paused by asking for
    reasons.
whatHappens:
  - You start a proceeding in the court.
  - You argue that one or more grounds of review apply.
  - 'The court looks at the legality of the decision, not its merits.'
  - 'If a ground is made out, the court can set the decision aside.'
remedies:
  - Set aside (quash) the decision.
  - 'Send it back (remit) to be decided again, lawfully.'
  - 'Orders to require or stop action, in some cases.'
  - >-
    A declaration — a formal statement of what the legal position is, without
    ordering anyone to do anything.
limits:
  - >-
    The court cannot substitute its own decision — it cannot simply give you the
    outcome you want, only require a lawful re-decision.
  - >-
    Judicial review is about legality, not about whether the decision was harsh
    or wrong on the facts.
goodToKnow:
  - >-
    A tribunal's own decision can be judicially reviewed. The tribunal acts
    under a law too, so a court can check that it kept within the law.
  - >-
    A court will not simply disagree with the result. It looks at whether the
    decision was made lawfully.
  - >-
    Judicial review can be technical and the time limits are tight — getting
    free legal help early really matters.
