---
id: unreasonableness
name: Legal unreasonableness
plainName: The decision was one no reasonable decision-maker could make
oneLine: >-
  Even with power, a decision can be unlawful if it lacks any sensible
  justification.
whatItMeans: >-
  Even where a decision-maker has the power, the decision can be unlawful if it
  lacks an evident and intelligible justification on the material before them,
  or is so unreasonable that no reasonable decision-maker could have reached it.
  This is a high bar — it is not enough to disagree.
plainExample: >-
  A penalty far out of proportion to a tiny, first-time mistake, with no
  explanation.
whatRelates:
  - The outcome seems to make no sense on the facts the decision-maker had.
  - The result is far harsher than the situation called for.
  - There is no intelligible reason given for the decision.
whatItIsNot: >-
  It does not mean you simply disagree with the decision — it has to lack a
  sensible basis.
usedIn:
  - judicial-review
test: >-
  A decision may be unlawful if it lacks an evident and intelligible
  justification, or is one no reasonable decision-maker could have reached.
elements:
  - id: justification
    name: No intelligible justification
    layPrompt: >-
      Does the outcome seem to make no sense on the facts the decision-maker
      had?
  - id: disproportionate
    name: Disproportionate or arbitrary
    layPrompt: >-
      Was the result far harsher than the situation called for, or seemingly
      arbitrary?
sources:

status: verified
leadingCases:

    pinpoint: ''

  - name: >-

    pinpoint: ''

  - name: Minister for Immigration and Citizenship v Li (2013) 249 CLR 332
    pinpoint: ''
    explains: >-
      The test used in Australia now. A decision needs a reason you can see

  - name: >-

    pinpoint: 'Kiefel CJ'

      The test is a stringent one. A court will not step in lightly where the
