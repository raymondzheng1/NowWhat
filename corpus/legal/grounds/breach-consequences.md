---
id: breach-consequences
name: Consequences of a breach
plainName: Does breaking the rule make the decision invalid?
oneLine: Not every mistake makes a decision invalid.
whatItMeans: >-
  Sometimes a rule was not followed. The next question is what that means. Some
  mistakes make a decision invalid. Others do not. It depends on what the Act
  intended, and on whether the mistake could have changed the outcome.
plainExample: >-
  An office missed a step it was meant to follow. Whether that undoes the
  decision depends on the Act.
whatRelates:
  - A step in the process was skipped.
  - You were not given something the rules say you get.
  - A time frame in the rules was not kept to.
  - Something went wrong that might have changed the answer.
whatItIsNot: >-
  This is not a ground on its own. It is the question that follows once
  something has gone wrong. It is also not a single rule for every mistake:
  a few problems are serious enough to count without asking whether they
  changed the outcome.
usedIn:
  - judicial-review
test: >-
  Whether a mistake makes a decision invalid depends on the purpose of the Act,
  and on whether the mistake could have made a difference. Some problems do not
  need that second step. Bias and unreasonableness count on their own.

This is the last question rather than the first. Something has gone wrong; whether it
undoes the decision is a separate matter, and one a free legal service is best placed to
work through with you.

Ground and authorities taken from our own verified judicial review materials.

Materiality exception added 2026-08-19. Our materials state the two-stage test with an express
carve-out — "bias/unreasonableness jurisdictional irrespective of materiality", and per LPDT those
two "deprive the decision of a character that status requires". The entry previously gave
materiality as though it applied to every error, which would tell someone their complaint fails
the outcome test when the law does not ask them to pass it. Bhardwaj added for the consequence our
materials attach to jurisdictional error: "a decision that involves jurisdictional error ... is
properly regarded, in law, as no decision at all".
