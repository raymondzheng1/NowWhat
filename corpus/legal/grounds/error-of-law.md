---
id: error-of-law
name: Error of law
plainName: They got the law wrong
oneLine: >-
  The decision-maker applied the wrong legal test, or misread what the law
  requires.
whatItMeans: >-
  A decision can be challenged where the decision-maker made a legal error — for
  example, applying the wrong test, misreading what a law requires, or
  misunderstanding a legal word. Some legal errors are reviewable even when they
  appear on the face of the written decision.
plainExample: A decision applies a stricter test than the law actually sets.
whatRelates:
  - The decision seems to apply the wrong rule or test.
  - The written reasons misstate what the law requires.
  - A legal word or requirement looks like it was misunderstood.
whatItIsNot: >-
  It does not cover a disagreement about the facts — it is about the law being
  applied wrongly.
usedIn:
  - judicial-review
test: >-
  An error of law happens where the decision-maker had the power but got the law
  wrong. That can mean misreading a word in the Act, using the wrong test, or
  misunderstanding the limits of the power. Some of these errors go to the power
  itself. Others show on the face of the reasons and may still be corrected.
elements:
  - id: wrong-test
    name: Wrong legal test
    layPrompt: Did they use the wrong legal test or standard?
  - id: misread-law
    name: Misreading the law
    layPrompt: Do the reasons misstate what the law requires?
sources:
  - Victoria Legal Aid — legalaid.vic.gov.au
status: verified
leadingCases:
  - name: Craig v South Australia (1995) 184 CLR 163
    pinpoint: at 175-180
    explains: >-
      Distinguishes errors of law within power from errors that take a
      decision-maker outside it.
  - name: Kirk v Industrial Court (NSW) (2010) 239 CLR 531
    pinpoint: ''
    explains: >-
      An error that shows on the face of the record can be corrected, even where
      it did not go to the power itself.
  - name: Collector of Customs v Agfa-Gevaert Ltd (1996) 186 CLR 389
    pinpoint: at 394-397
    explains: Where the line runs between a question of fact and a question of law.

Test rewritten 2026-08-19. It previously read "An error of law (including one on the face of the
record) may be reviewable", which restates the ground's name rather than saying what it is. Our
materials describe it as a decision-maker who "has power to act, but has exercised it on an
erroneous legal basis", typically "by misconstruing a statutory term, applying the wrong legal
test, or misunderstanding the legal limits of their power". Kirk added alongside Craig for the
record point.
