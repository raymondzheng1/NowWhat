---
id: illogicality
name: Illogical or irrational reasoning
plainName: The reasons do not follow
oneLine: A conclusion has to follow from what the decision-maker had.
whatItMeans: >-
  A decision can be wrong in the way it reasons. Not merely arguable, but with
  no sensible path from the material to the answer. This is close to
  unreasonableness. It looks at the steps rather than the outcome.
plainExample: >-
  The letter accepts your doctor's report. It then says you have no health
  problem.
whatRelates:
  - Two things the letter says cannot both be true.
  - The letter accepts something and then decides the opposite.
  - A conclusion appears with no step in between.
  - The reasons rely on something the letter itself rules out.
whatItIsNot: >-
  This is not about the decision being harsh, or about you disagreeing. It is
  about reasoning that does not hold together.
usedIn:
  - judicial-review
test: >-
  A conclusion reached with no logical or rational path from the material is not
  a proper exercise of the power.
elements:
  - id: contradiction
    name: A step that does not follow
    layPrompt: Do two parts of the letter contradict each other?
  - id: no-path
    name: A missing step
    layPrompt: Does the letter jump to a conclusion with nothing in between?
sources:
  - Owner's administrative-law knowledge base (AdminLawCoach corpus, 2026-08-17)
status: verified
leadingCases:
  - name: >-
      Minister for Immigration and Citizenship v SZMDS (2010) 240 CLR 611
    pinpoint: ''
    explains: >-
      Reasoning with no logical path to its conclusion can make a decision
      invalid.
  - name: >-
      Minister for Immigration and Citizenship v Li (2013) 249 CLR 332
    pinpoint: 'at 367 [76]'
    explains: A decision needs a reason you can see and follow.
---

This ground is about the steps, not the result. It is closely tied to unreasonableness,
and in practice the two are often raised together.
