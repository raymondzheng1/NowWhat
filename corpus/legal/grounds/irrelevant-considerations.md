---
id: irrelevant-considerations
name: Irrelevant considerations
plainName: They relied on something that should not have mattered
oneLine: The law can also name things a decision-maker must leave out.
whatItMeans: >-
  Some Acts say what a decision-maker may not take into account. An Act does not
  always spell that out — it can also follow from what the Act is for. Relying
  on a forbidden matter can make a decision unlawful. This point often travels
  with a claim about the wrong purpose.
plainExample: >-
  A fine is upheld partly because you complained about the officer. Your
  complaint had nothing to do with the fine.
whatRelates:
  - The reasons mention something that has nothing to do with the rules.
  - Your past, your attitude, or an old complaint seems to have counted.
  - A staff member raised a topic the rules never mention.
  - The outcome seems driven by something outside the scheme.
whatItIsNot: >-
  It does not cover every fact you think was unfair. The law must actually put
  that matter out of bounds.
usedIn:
  - judicial-review
test: >-
  An Act may forbid certain matters from being taken into account. It can say so
  outright, or it can be implied from what the Act is for. Relying on a
  forbidden matter can make the decision invalid.
elements:
  - id: forbidden-matter
    name: A matter the law puts out of bounds
    layPrompt: Do the rules say this topic should not affect the decision?
  - id: was-relied-on
    name: The decision-maker relied on it
    layPrompt: Does that topic look like it counted towards the decision?
sources:
  - Owner's administrative-law knowledge base (AdminLawCoach corpus, 2026-08-16)
status: verified
leadingCases:
  - name: Minister for Aboriginal Affairs v Peko-Wallsend Ltd (1986) 162 CLR 24
    pinpoint: ''
    explains: >-
      The same Act can also imply which matters must be left out.
---

This ground is the mirror of relevant considerations. There, something the law required was left out. Here, something the law rules out was let in. One asks what was missing. The other asks what should never have been there.

Provenance. This ground is drawn from our own verified judicial review materials. The cases it cites come from the same source. Verified on 2026-08-16. A supervising lawyer sign-off is tracked separately.

**"Cost or convenience" prompt deleted 2026-08-23 on the owner's ruling.** The entry invited a
reader to treat a decision driven by cost or convenience as a sign of this ground. Nothing in the
owner's materials makes cost a forbidden matter — they support only the Peko-Wallsend rule that the
Act itself must expressly state, or necessarily imply, that a matter must or must not be taken into
account.

The prompt was worse than merely unsourced. In several of the schemes this audience faces, cost is a
lawful and sometimes a mandatory consideration, so the line pointed people at a ground the law may
not give them. The first prompt — "The reasons mention something that has nothing to do with the
rules" — already carries the sourced idea, so nothing was lost by removing it.

**Implication and reliance added 2026-08-23.** Two gaps, both visible against the mirror entry.
`test` and `whatItMeans` said only that an Act may forbid a matter, while relevant-considerations
has published "It can say so outright, or it can be implied from what the Act is for" since 19
August. Peko-Wallsend runs both ways — an Act may "expressly state, or necessarily imply, that a
certain consideration must (or must not) be taken into account" — and this entry's own case note
already carried that, while the two fields stating the rule did not. A reader who cannot find a
forbidden matter written down would have dropped the point.

The `was-relied-on` prompt asked "Does that topic appear in the reasons you were given?". A mention
is not reliance, and the element it sits under is called "The decision-maker relied on it". The
prompt now asks whether the topic looks like it counted towards the decision, which is the question
the element was always meant to put.
